The [h] Trick — How to Calculate
Total Hours in Excel

=TEXT(B2-A2,"[h]:mm")

Most people don't know that Excel's regular time format caps out at 24 hours. The [h] bracket tells Excel to show total hours — so 26:30 instead of 2:30. Essential for timesheets, shift logs, and any multi-day tracking.

SOLOPRENEUR Freelancers & creators

Track billable client hours

Log start and end times for each client session across multiple days. Sum the results with SUM — and this formula keeps the total accurate even when it exceeds 24 hours.

Total client hours this week:
=TEXT(SUM(C2:C10),"[h]:mm")
OFFICE Teams & small business

Weekly shift hour totals

Calculate total hours worked across a week of shifts. Staff who work more than 24 hours in a week will show the wrong number without [h] — this formula fixes that silently.

Shift duration (end − start):
=TEXT(D2-C2,"[h]:mm")

Why the [h] bracket matters

❌ Without [h] — broken

=TEXT(B2-A2,"h:mm")

Excel resets after 24 hours. So 26 hours worked shows as 2:00 — completely wrong.

✅ With [h] — correct

=TEXT(B2-A2,"[h]:mm")

The square brackets tell Excel to accumulate hours beyond 24. 26 hours shows as 26:00.

Copy-paste ready versions:

Single row duration: =TEXT(B2-A2,"[h]:mm")
Sum a column of times: =TEXT(SUM(C2:C20),"[h]:mm")
With "hrs" label: =TEXT(B2-A2,"[h]:mm")&" hrs"
